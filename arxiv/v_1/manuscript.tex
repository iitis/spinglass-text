\begin{abstract}
This update consolidates the four separately registered packages of
\texttt{SpinGlassPEPS.jl} into one installable package with a curated public API.
Truncating factorizations now report the weight they discard, so a heuristic
contraction can state how much it discarded without an exact oracle, and an
inverse-temperature ladder with warm-started boundary matrix product states makes
the choice of $\beta$ empirical. Profiling shows the solver is host-bound; moving
the contraction temporaries off the collected heap cuts a 2048-spin bond-32
solve's allocated memory by two-thirds (90.9 to 32~GiB on CPU). On an H100 system
we also locate where GPU execution begins to pay, later than the original article
implies.
\end{abstract}

Ising model \sep reproducibility

\section{Description of the software-update}
\label{Description of the software-update}

\textbf{Motivation.} The original publication~\cite{smierzchalski2025spinglasspeps}
described \texttt{SpinGlassPEPS.jl} as separately registered
Julia~\cite{bezanson2017julia} packages in version lockstep, whose interface had
since drifted: renames shipped without deprecation paths, so the listings printed
in the 2025 article no longer ran. Worse, the solver reported no measure of how
much its heuristic contraction discarded, so a result could be validated only
against an exact oracle, as the original benchmark had to do when it certified
ground states with CPLEX. Algorithms and an extensive benchmark
campaign are described separately~\cite{dziubyna2024limitations}, and this version
is archived on Zenodo~\cite{spinglasspeps_zenodo}.

\textbf{Consolidation into one package.} The four implementation packages
\texttt{SpinGlassTensors}, \texttt{SpinGlassNetworks},
\texttt{SpinGlassExhaustive} and \texttt{SpinGlassEngine} are now internal modules
of a single installable \texttt{SpinGlassPEPS}, so \texttt{pkg> add SpinGlassPEPS}
installs the complete solver. \texttt{using SpinGlassPEPS} brings a curated surface
of roughly eighty documented symbols instead of re-exporting some 240. Lower-level
kernels remain reachable through the submodules, and deprecation shims restore the
API forms printed in the original article, so its listings execute again.

The clustered Hamiltonian is now a concrete \texttt{PottsHamiltonian} type with
typed fields read through accessors, rather than a \texttt{MetaGraphs} property bag
consulted dynamically inside the search loop. The process-global memoization that
cached boundary matrix product states (MPS), MPO layers and environments has been
replaced by a contraction cache owned by each contractor, with explicit per-row
eviction. The old design was thread-unsafe by construction and had to be cleared by
manual calls scattered through the search, so concurrent solving was not available
before this change.

The update also repairs defects that silently affected results.
\texttt{corner\_matrix} threw for every \texttt{SiteTensor} input and returned
permuted trailing dimensions for \texttt{VirtualTensor}, and the exhaustive GPU
search launched too few blocks,
truncating the reported spectrum for problems larger than about nine spins. The
benchmark instances at the size used by the original article's figures are now
shipped with the package, since the published listing resolved them from a directory
that did not contain them.

\textbf{Contraction error control.} Every truncating factorization now records the
relative weight it discards, $\varepsilon_i =
\lVert\Sigma_{\text{dropped}}\rVert^2/\lVert\Sigma\rVert^2$, into an accumulator
scoped to the running task, so concurrent solves cannot pool statistics. A solve
reports $\sum_i\varepsilon_i$, the leading term of the accumulated fidelity loss of
a boundary-MPS sweep, together with $\max_i\varepsilon_i$. It also counts the
truncations in which the bond-dimension bound, rather than the singular-value
tolerance, forced a non-negligible discard, and it records how many dimensions were
retained out of those offered. Those last two quantities were not available in the
original release. On a 128-spin instance, bond dimension $D=4$ gives
$\sum\varepsilon = 3.1\times10^{-4}$ with all 18 truncations forced by the bond
bound, retaining 108 of 307 singular values. At $D=32$ the same solve gives
$5.6\times10^{-14}$ with no truncation bond-limited, retaining 192 of 592. A
bond-limited count of zero means that no bond-capped truncation discarded relative
weight above the $10^{-12}$ reporting threshold, so no change from raising $D$ is
expected at the reported precision. The original release gave no way to check that.

Across the eight-transformation protocol the solver additionally reports the spread
of energies and how many transformations reached the best one. Agreement among eight
distinct transformed contraction orders is an oracle-free consistency check. A
spread that is a sizeable fraction of the energy scale flags
transformation-order-sensitive disagreement, which may reflect contraction or search
error.

\textbf{An inverse-temperature ladder.} $\beta$ is the most consequential
parameter and was also the least guidable. If it is too small, the conditional
probabilities the search branches on say little about the ground state. The
original interface nonetheless took a scalar whose optimum its own documentation
conceded to be instance-dependent. \texttt{beta\_ladder} walks an increasing
schedule in which each rung reuses the previous rung's boundary MPS to start
variational compression, instead of forming $W\psi$ exactly and truncating it.
Where boundary-MPS construction dominates (2048-spin instance) a warm-started rung
is ${\approx}25\%$ faster for identical energies (median paired cold/warm ratios of
$1.32$ and $1.34$ over five interleaved rounds, or ${\approx}16\%$ over the whole
ladder). The gain is bounded by the share of the solve spent building boundary
MPS, so it is smaller wherever the search dominates instead.

The error measure cannot be pushed arbitrarily far. Warm starting is incompatible
with it, because a warm start optimizes \emph{within} a fixed bond dimension and
never truncates, so it reports $\sum\varepsilon\approx0$ whatever its accuracy, its
error being a variational gap that the log cannot see. Audits therefore have to use
cold builds, and the package warns when they do not. A second limitation is that
$\sum\varepsilon$ does not rank solution quality. In Figure~\ref{fig:evr}(c) the
energy error falls monotonically with $\beta$, reaching the best energy found in
9 of 10 instances at $\beta=6$, while $\sum\varepsilon$ peaks at $\beta=4$ and
then falls, because a sharply peaked Boltzmann distribution carries less
entanglement and truncates more easily. Discarded weight therefore does not order
solution quality: $\beta=2$ carries almost the lowest $\sum\varepsilon$ of any
setting measured yet gives the worst energy, while $\beta=4$ carries the highest
and does better. The quantity bounds what the contraction discarded. It says
nothing about whether the search found a good state.

Figure~\ref{fig:evr}(d) shows that search breadth does not buy diversity. At
$M=1024$, forty-nine of the fifty runs returned a single valley, with states
differing from the best by tens of spins in most runs and never by more than $399$
out of $2500$, where a diversity count would demand $625$. The one exception
($\beta{=}8$, instance~1) is a degraded contraction rather than a genuine second
optimum (Figure~\ref{fig:evr} caption). The metric of the original article scores
the exact global spin flip as two solutions. These instances carry no local fields,
so $E(\sigma)=E(-\sigma)$ identically, and the mirror sits at distance $N$ for free.
Changing the contraction order does move the solver between valleys. One state from
each of the eight transformations gives two or three mutually distant solutions on
four of five instances, with pairwise distances reaching $1249$ of a possible
$1250$. This argues for sweeping the transformations on grounds independent of
energy, and it holds even where the transformations agree on the energy to several
digits.

\textbf{Concurrent transformation sweeps.} The standard protocol solves each
instance once per lattice transformation, a loop that previously lived in user code.
\texttt{sweep\_transformations} now owns that loop and seeds each transformation
deterministically, which matters because the \texttt{Zipper} strategy draws a random
sketch and results would otherwise depend on task scheduling. It also isolates
failures and reports the diagnostics described above. The limit on fanning out is
device memory, which depends on the instance rather than on the machine, so
concurrency is gated by a byte budget. A calibration solve measures peak usage, and
each admitted solve carries its reservation as a task-scoped value, so kernel
batches are sized against \emph{its} slice rather than against the shared pool.
Without that last step the reservation would be advisory only, because every
concurrent solve would measure the same free pool and batch as though it owned all
of it.

Concurrency pays on both devices in Table~\ref{tab:sweep}. On CPU the sweep
scales monotonically, reaching $3.1\times$ and $1.6\times$ at eight-way on the two
cases. On a single GPU it helps as well, up to $1.7\times$ at two-way and
$1.4\times$ at four-way. A single admitted solve ($c{=}1$) is a slight net loss,
because the sweep's fixed set-up cost is not amortized when nothing overlaps, and the
gain therefore begins at $c{\ge}2$. Energies agree across every admitted solve.

One point of method deserves mention, because it cost us a wrong result before we
caught it. Timing the serial arm immediately after a concurrent warm-up leaves the
CUDA memory pool in a state that inflated our serial baseline by up to $2.7\times$,
which is enough to distort any speed-up ratio measured against it. We therefore
interleave the two arms and reclaim fully before each timed section. The ratios we
quote are paired per-round ratios, and any performance claim about this solver needs
the same care.

\textbf{When the GPU is worth using.} The original article recommends the GPU for
its larger examples without qualification. Solving identical configurations on each
device (Figure~\ref{fig:cross}, Table~\ref{tab:cross}) shows that this holds only
above a threshold that is easy to fall below. The host is ${\approx}45\times$ faster
at 36 spins and, at 2048 spins and bond 16, still ${\approx}1.6\times$ faster in the
dense case and ${\approx}1.3\times$ faster in the sparse one. The device overtakes it
only in the largest sparse case, 2048 spins at bond 32, where the GPU is
${\approx}1.4\times$ faster. The dense case at that size still favours the host by
${\approx}10\%$, and energies agree in every cell. On the tested Xeon/H100 system the
CPU is therefore preferable for exploratory work in the smaller or dense
configurations, and the device is worth using in the D-Wave-scale sparse regime at
large bond.

\textbf{Where the time goes.} Profiling on the tested RTX~5080 configuration
explains both tables and redirects the optimizations one would otherwise attempt
first. On the profiled 128-spin solve the bottleneck was the host rather than the
device. Host-side CUDA API calls account for only about a quarter of wall time, and
most of the rest is host-side Julia work. Being host-bound helps to explain why
the CPU remains competitive across the measured crossover. Accelerating the tensor work removes none
of the host-side bookkeeping.

An idle device invites kernel-level batching. We did not pursue it, because
eliminating the entire CUDA API share would cap the gain at ${\approx}1.3\times$,
whereas the host allocation load was reachable directly. One line,
\texttt{branch\_states} in the branch-and-bound search, accounted for 52.7\% of the
bytes the profiled 128-spin solve allocated, and two changes follow from that
(Figure~\ref{fig:alloc}). The branched-configuration set is now backed by a single
matrix rather than tens of thousands of small vectors per call, cutting the number
of live objects sharply. Its allocated \emph{bytes} fall only ${\approx}4\%$ on the
large solve's device arm and ${\approx}1\%$ on its host arm, but the object count
that drives collection falls far more.
Separately, the multi-tensor contraction temporaries were moved off the collected
heap onto explicit allocation, cutting a 2048-spin bond-32 solve's allocated bytes
by about two thirds (90.9 to 32~GiB on CPU). The post-change allocation totals were
reproduced on both tested systems, and both reductions were measured in a full solve,
since the explicit allocator is no faster in isolation and pays only through the
allocations it removes from the collected heap.

\textbf{Impact, limitations and outlook.} Contraction error is now observable,
which lets a practitioner detect substantial contraction loss on a problem where no
exact answer is available, and that is the regime the method is intended for. The
measure diagnoses the contraction, and it does not by itself establish overall
solver convergence or solution quality. The consolidation and the deprecation shims
make the published interface reproducible, and shipping the benchmark instances
closes a gap in that reproducibility that predates this update. The raw per-run
data and figure scripts behind the figures and tables, together with runnable
drivers for the crossover, concurrency, diversity, energy-vs-runtime,
contraction-error and $\beta$-ladder results, are provided as supplementary
material accompanying this article in the \texttt{benchmarks/} directory. The allocation figure reports allocated
bytes. Its post-change values were reproduced on both tested systems, while its
pre-change values are recorded measurements of the superseded code.

Several limitations remain. Warm starting and the discarded-weight measure cannot be
combined, and warm starting currently covers only the bottom boundary MPS, so
layouts that build the top boundary do not benefit on those rows. The error measure
also answers a narrower question than one might hope. It bounds what the contraction
discarded, and our own attempt to use it for selecting $\beta$ failed, which is why
the package now documents it as a diagnostic rather than a criterion.

The remaining headroom is on the host side rather than in the tensor kernels. The
branch-and-bound bookkeeping still dominates allocation even after the change
described above, and the sparse contraction path, which is where the device wins
according to Table~\ref{tab:cross}, has not received the allocator treatment that
the dense one now has. Both look more promising than the kernel batching that an
idle device suggests.
